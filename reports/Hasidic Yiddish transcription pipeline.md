# Rent Yiddish transcription, own the timestamps

The best path is to rent transcription instead of training a model, and to put the team's effort into the one piece no vendor sells: a small pipeline in which an AI model writes headings and summaries that point to numbered lines of the transcript, while software takes every time from the audio itself and snaps each cut to a natural pause. A cheaper, good-enough engine does exist. In the only head-to-head test of Hasidic Yiddish, **Yiddish Tools made 4.7 errors per 100 words against 2.9 for YiddishLabs**, the reportedly expensive leader, at list prices of about **$20–$270 a month** for 100 hours a week if its terms allow hotline use; Google's Gemini made about 13, and unmodified Whisper and the many apps built on it were unusable at 93–110 ([3Yiddish benchmark](https://github.com/TripleU613/3Yiddish/blob/main/BENCHMARK.md)). With AI summaries and a timing pass included, the recommended setup runs about **$17–$97 a week ($74–$420 a month)**, against roughly $135 a week for Sofer.ai's turnkey service, an unpublished YiddishLabs bill, and at least $3,750 a week for human transcribers; the larger cost is a one-time build of roughly four to eight developer-weeks. Training your own model works, and a Chabad project cut word errors from 57% to 8% for a single speaker ([rebbe-yiddish-asr](https://github.com/golanbenoni/rebbe-yiddish-asr)), but a minimal version would cost about **$30,000–$119,000 in its first year** counting staff time ($5,000–$15,500 in cash) and pays back only against an engine charging about $6 or more an hour, so it should wait. The whole system is **achievable now as a staff assistant**, an outline that staff skim, click and nudge before clips export, but not as hands-off automatic clipping. Because every accuracy figure rests on one small test (25 clips, one speaker, no shiur audio, built by the author of a rival tool), the first step is a two-to-four-week bake-off on 10–15 of the hotline's own lectures.

## The recommended setup runs $17–$97 a week at 100 hours

The recommendation has four parts: three rented, one owned. A specialist engine turns each lecture into Yiddish text. **Yiddish Tools is the first choice**: it scored second only to YiddishLabs in the only Hasidic test, publishes the lowest prices among the accurate specialists, and returns a time for every word ([Yiddish Tools](https://yiddish.tools/)). Gemini is the mainstream fallback, and YiddishLabs earns the job only if a volume quote comes in low. A free open Yiddish speech model, run on a rented GPU, supplies an independent clock taken from the audio ([ivrit.ai yi-whisper](https://huggingface.co/ivrit-ai/yi-whisper-large-v3)). A general AI model reads the numbered transcript and proposes chapters, headings and summaries; Claude Sonnet 5.5 is used in the budget below, but a blind staff test should make the final pick. The owned part is a small program and review page that ties these together, converts chapter line numbers into audio times, snaps cuts to pauses and exports phone-ready clips.

Every path below is normalized to the hotline's volume of 100 hours a week, which is 433 hours a month and 5,200 hours a year. The accuracy column comes from the single small benchmark described in the third section (25 clips, one speaker, not shiur audio, built by a rival tool's author). Only the first row and Sofer.ai include headings and summaries; to any other row, add about **$12–$34 a week ($54–$149 a month)** for AI summaries and a timing pass.

The recommended row's low end assumes Yiddish Tools' consumer plan covers hotline use; built on Gemini instead, the same stack costs about $22–$74 a week.

Treat every vendor price as the opening line of a quote, not a bill. Most were read from search-engine extracts or third-party price trackers because the vendors' own pages could not be opened during the research, and the Sofer.ai extract was garbled enough that its $1.50 overage might belong to a different plan. **YiddishLabs publishes no dollar price per credit at all**; its row borrows Yiddish Tools' rate of $1 per 1,000 credits purely as a yardstick. Several facts about Yiddish Tools, YiddishLabs and Heimish Geredt come from the Torah & Yiddish AI directory, a well-sourced community file whose maintainer builds some of the tools it lists. And commercial terms are unresolved for the two strongest affordable options: Yiddish Tools steers business use toward an "unlimited commercial license" priced only by contract, and Sofer.ai permits commercial use of transcripts only for paid subscribers "who have received explicit written permission" ([Sofer.ai terms](https://app.sofer.ai/terms)).

The bills are small enough that the build, not the subscription, dominates the first year. Wiring the pieces together and building a review page is ordinary software work rather than research, but it is not free: my estimate is **four to eight weeks of a capable developer's time**, about $12,000–$56,000 at an assumed contractor rate of $3,000–$7,000 a week, and much less if someone on staff builds it. A first version that only produces the timed outline, leaving staff to cut in their usual audio editor, is a smaller job; the waveform page and automatic export can follow. The same arithmetic clarifies Sofer.ai's place. If its outlines pass the bake-off on the hotline's own audio, its roughly $7,000 a year beats paying a contractor to build for several years, though the community reports in the third section suggest its Hasidic Yiddish will struggle.

The choice of AI model for headings barely moves the budget. At list prices, one outline pass over an hour of lecture costs under a cent on Claude Haiku 5.5 and about 29 cents on Claude Opus 5.5, so a month of 433 hours runs from about **$3 to about $125**, halved by Anthropic's 50% batch discount for overnight work ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). The budget's two-pass Sonnet 5.5 setup, which writes chapters and then checks them, comes to about $89 a month, or $44 in batch; OpenAI's GPT-5.4 mini lands near $19 a month ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-5-4-mini-and-nano/)). These figures rest on an estimate of roughly 42,000 input tokens per lecture hour for Claude, whose tokenizer Anthropic does not publish, so the hotline should measure real transcripts with the vendors' free token counters before committing. One trap matters: Haiku 5.5 bills an entire request at five times its base rate once the prompt passes 100,000 tokens, which at that estimate means splitting any lecture longer than about two and a half hours. With a spread this narrow, quality alone should pick the model, and same-day clipping may justify paying full price for part of the volume instead of waiting for batch results.

## Achievable now as a staff assistant, not as hands-off clipping

The project is achievable, in the form of an assistant rather than an autopilot. Every link in the chain exists: an engine that made fewer than five errors per 100 words in the one small heimish test, a timing method that commercial transcription companies already use, AI summarization that costs at most about 30 cents per lecture hour, standard tools for cutting audio, and open-source parts for a review page. What does not exist is one product that runs the whole chain for Hasidic Yiddish. Sofer.ai comes closest, generating a timestamped topic outline and summaries for every transcript and exporting clips ([Jewish Link](https://jewishlink.news/from-the-people-of-the-book-to-the-people-of-the-transcript-powered-by-sofer-ai/)), but its Hasidic accuracy is unproven. General tools fall away entirely: Descript transcribes only Latin-alphabet languages ([Descript](https://help.descript.com/hc/en-us/articles/10249408168845)), and Spotify's automatic chapters work only in English ([Spotify](https://support.spotify.com/us/creators/article/episode-chapters/)).

Three things remain unproven, and each argues for a human checkpoint. **Nobody has measured any engine on real shiur audio.** The benchmark used short takes of free speech, while lectures are long, fast, dense with citations, broken by niggunim that send Whisper-based models into repetition loops ([directory, on Sicha to Text](https://github.com/pinta-partners/torah-yiddish-ai-directory)), and often recorded in noisy halls. One data point shows the likely drop: the ivrit.ai model that scored 18.3 errors per 100 on the benchmark scored about **57% word error on full recordings** of the Lubavitcher Rebbe's farbrengens ([rebbe-yiddish-asr](https://github.com/golanbenoni/rebbe-yiddish-asr)). The scoring differs, but the direction does not. **Nobody has tested a frontier AI model on Yiddish either**: the only Yiddish language benchmark, Kashes, covered open models of 8–9 billion parameters and reported no results for GPT, Gemini or Claude ([MameLoshnLM/Kashes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05850)). And **where one topic ends and the next begins is a judgment call** even for the best systems. On YouTube videos, a research model trained for the task scores 45 out of 100 on boundary F1, a measure of how often its chapter breaks land near the ones people chose, and Gemini 1.5 Pro scores 42 untrained ([Chapter-Llama](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00072)).

The realistic workflow therefore puts staff in the loop at the cheapest possible point. Each lecture arrives as an outline of headings, two-line summaries, start and end times, a clip-worthiness score and warning flags, shown beside a waveform and a clickable Yiddish transcript. Staff read the outline, play the first and last five seconds of each proposed clip, drag an edge where needed and press accept, after which the system cuts and exports on its own; cutting by hand remains possible from the same page. How many listening hours this saves has not been measured for any comparable workflow, so the pilot should time it. A staged plan keeps every commitment small until that evidence arrives.

| Step | Calendar | What happens | Cash cost | Move on when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Bake-off | 2–4 weeks | Staff write reference outlines (headings and start times) for 10–15 of their own lectures, mixing speakers and audio quality. Each lecture runs through Yiddish Tools, Gemini, Sofer.ai and a paid YiddishLabs sample; 3–5 AI models write outlines from the same transcripts, rated blind by two staff | Mostly trial credits and API fees (estimate: a few hundred dollars at most); optional word-for-word reference transcripts at an estimated $216–$630 per audio hour | One engine-and-model pair gets headings right and lands clip starts on the right sentence |
| 2. Build | 1–2 months | Timed-outline pipeline first, then review page and phone export | ~4–8 developer-weeks (estimate) | Staff accept most proposed clips with small nudges |
| 3. Run and log | Ongoing | Every acceptance, nudge and rejection is logged; the glossary of terms grows | $74–$420 a month | Monthly re-scoring against the reference outlines |
| 4. Reconsider fine-tuning | After 3–6 months of logs | Only if logs show the engine failing on key speakers or terms, or vendor terms block hotline use | $5,000–$15,500 cash; $30,000–$119,000 with staff time | Decided by the logs |

The bake-off is cheap because most of it runs on trials. Yiddish Tools' 5,000 starter credits cover about eight hours of audio ([Yiddish Tools](https://yiddish.tools/)), Gemini costs well under a dollar per audio hour ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)), and Sofer.ai's top plan is listed at $72 for a month ([Sofer.ai pricing](https://www.sofer.ai/#pricing)). YiddishLabs is the exception: its 5,000 starter credits, per the community directory ([directory](https://github.com/pinta-partners/torah-yiddish-ai-directory)), buy only about 28 minutes at 180 credits a minute ([YiddishLabs credits](https://app.yiddishlabs.com/credits)), so a fair test means buying credits, which also reveals the real price. The pass mark should be set before testing and stated in the hotline's own terms: the share of headings staff judge correct, factual errors per summary, how far each proposed start sits from the staff's own, and the share of clips staff would cut as proposed. Raw word-error rates are a poor guide here, because the choice of spelling and Unicode form alone moved one Yiddish system's measured error from 38% to nearly 100% on the same audio ([Bleaman, LREC 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.htres-2.3/)).

## The good-enough engine is a cheap specialist, with Gemini as backup

Only one test compares Hasidic Yiddish engines side by side, and its limits shape every conclusion in this section. The 3Yiddish benchmark, posted to GitHub between 15 and 23 November 2026, ran 21 tools on **25 takes of free heimish speech from one speaker, 1,191 words in all**, counting wrong words per 100 and, separately, misses among 13 loshon-kodesh words, 14 English words and 3 names ([3Yiddish benchmark](https://github.com/TripleU613/3Yiddish/blob/main/BENCHMARK.md)).

| Engine | Errors per 100 words | Loshon-kodesh words missed (of 13) | English words missed (of 14) | Perfect takes (of 25) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| YiddishLabs (paid) | **2.9** | 1 | 0 | 14 |
| Yiddish Tools (paid) | **4.7** | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| 3Yiddish, Whisper-based (free, non-commercial) | 11.9 | 6 | 10 | 3 |
| Gemini 4 "Carbon" via OpenRouter | 13.2 | 5 | 2 | 2 |
| Gemini 4 "Argon" via OpenRouter | 13.6 | 4 | 2 | 1 |
| ivrit.ai turbo (free, open) | 15.6 | 10 | 13 | 2 |
| ivrit.ai large-v3 (free, open) | 18.3 | 11 | 8 | 5 |
| Heimish Geredt (paid) | 32.9 | 11 | 6 | 0 |
| Whisper large-v3, unmodified | 92.9 | 13 | 14 | 0 |

The test's author builds a competing free tool, 3Yiddish, and says the answer key grew out of 3Yiddish's own draft and that recent entries were scored partly by AI agents following the speaker's judgments, choices that if anything favour 3Yiddish and the ivrit.ai models it descends from. That he still ranks two paid rivals first lends the top of the table some credibility. But one person speaking freely into a microphone is not a maggid shiur in a crowded beis medrash, and no take is long enough to expose the drift and looping that dominate long recordings.

For a hotline that needs headings rather than verbatim text, the sub-scores matter more than the headline number, because headings rest on exactly the words engines miss: the sefer, the pasuk, the commentator, the rebbe. **The free ivrit.ai models missed 10–11 of 13 loshon-kodesh words**, which would leave a summarizer guessing at the topic; Gemini missed 4–5, Yiddish Tools 2 and YiddishLabs 1. Gemini's strength is English, with only 2 of 14 English words missed against 8–13 for the open models, which helps with lectures that slide into English. YiddishLabs' edge over Yiddish Tools looks small in the headline but shows in perfect takes, 14 of 25 against 4. And cheap does not mean usable: Heimish Geredt, listed at $4 a month ([directory](https://github.com/pinta-partners/torah-yiddish-ai-directory)), stopped early or skipped a stretch in six takes, and YidKey replaced whole stretches with words that were never said ([3Yiddish benchmark](https://github.com/TripleU613/3Yiddish/blob/main/BENCHMARK.md)), failures far more dangerous to a summary than misspellings.

The Gemini figure needs particular care. Google publishes no Yiddish audio results, and the search of mainstream speech services turned up no independent test either; the 3Yiddish benchmark is the lone exception and the only basis for the score of about 13. Its limits go beyond the benchmark's general ones. The two models that scored about 13 belong to a Gemini 4 family whose prices only third-party trackers report ([Maximem](https://www.maximem.ai/blog/gemini-api-pricing-2026)); two sibling Gemini 4 models failed to return transcripts on about half the takes; and **the current Flash models priced in this report were not tested at all**. Google does list Yiddish among the languages its general Gemini models handle, but that list carries no promise about audio ([Vertex AI models](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/models)), and its dedicated transcription products, Chirp 3 and Gemini 3.5 Transcribe, do not list Yiddish; Gemini 3.5 Transcribe still deserves an hour of testing because it returns genuine word timestamps ([Gemini transcription docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/transcribe)). The lineup also churns: Google is restricting the 2.5 models to existing users ([Gemini changelog](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/changelog)), and one tracker reports Gemini 3.8 Flash's price doubling in January 2027 ([MorphLLM](https://www.morphllm.com/gemini-api-pricing)). Gemini is a credible backup whose real performance the bake-off must establish, run on the paid tier because Google uses free-tier data to improve its products ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)).

Among the specialists, terms matter as much as accuracy. **YiddishLabs** leads on accuracy and offers a developer API, but charges 180 credits per audio minute without publishing what a credit costs ([YiddishLabs credits](https://app.yiddishlabs.com/credits)), documents no timestamp feature, and is probably the expensive tool the hotline has already met: forum users say it "worked pretty well but is expensive" ([JTech Forums](https://forums.jtechforums.org/t/yiddish-transcription-through-ai/5937)). **Yiddish Tools** pairs the second-best score with a time for every word, offline Windows programs, a community dictionary of more than 144,000 Hasidic Yiddish words, and an unusual option: a commercial license that hands over the model weights to run on your own servers, listed at $1,499 for the large dictation model at its October 2026 launch ([directory](https://github.com/pinta-partners/torah-yiddish-ai-directory)). Its consumer plans, $19.99–$39.99 a month with a cap of 24 audio hours a day against the hotline's 14, look like a bargain, but the homepage steers business use toward a contract license, and the directory and homepage disagree about whether a hosted API exists ([Yiddish Tools](https://yiddish.tools/)), which matters because automating 10–20 lectures a day needs one. **Sofer.ai** is the one turnkey choice, built for shiurim that mix English, Hebrew, Aramaic and Yiddish, though its dedicated Yiddish mode is still in beta ([Sofer.ai](https://www.sofer.ai/)); it offers no public API, and users call it "cheaper but did a sloppy job" on Yiddish ([JTech Forums](https://forums.jtechforums.org/t/yiddish-transcription-through-ai/5937)) and rate it about 6 out of 10 ([directory, citing ChabadLabs](https://github.com/pinta-partners/torah-yiddish-ai-directory)).

The free options sort into one useful base and several dead ends. ivrit.ai's Yiddish Whisper models are Apache-licensed, free for commercial use and the foundation of most Yiddish speech work ([ivrit.ai](https://huggingface.co/ivrit-ai/yi-whisper-large-v3)); their text is too weak on loshon-kodesh to carry headings, but their timestamps come from the audio, which makes them the natural clock for the pipeline. 3Yiddish's own model scored better, at 11.9, but carries a non-commercial license whose author writes "Don't sell it. Ever." ([3Yiddish](https://github.com/TripleU613/3Yiddish)); whether a hotline's use counts as commercial is a question for the author or a lawyer. Everything built on unmodified Whisper, from TurboScribe to a long tail of transcription websites, inherits its 93–110 errors per 100 and should be ruled out. Among other mainstream services, Gladia alone lists Yiddish in its official language table, with no quality evidence ([Gladia](https://docs.gladia.io/chapters/language/supported-languages)); ElevenLabs' Yiddish setting returns Latin-letter transliteration instead of Hebrew script ([ElevenLabs issue #508](https://github.com/elevenlabs/elevenlabs-js/issues/508)); and Deepgram, Amazon and others support Hebrew but not Yiddish ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/blog/speech-to-text-for-hebrew-persian-urdu-on-nova-3); [AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/transcribe/latest/dg/supported-languages.html)).

## Fine-tuning would swap a $20–$270 monthly bill for a $30,000 project

Fine-tuning works, and the best evidence comes from inside the Hasidic world. The rebbe-yiddish-asr project took ivrit.ai's Yiddish model, which scored about 57% word error on full recordings of the Lubavitcher Rebbe's farbrengens, and retrained it on 153 hours of those recordings matched to published transcripts; **word error fell to 8.0%**, with character error at 4.5%, on 20 held-out days ([rebbe-yiddish-asr](https://github.com/golanbenoni/rebbe-yiddish-asr)). Studies of other low-resource languages show the same shape, with the steepest gains in the first 50 hours of in-domain labeled audio and diminishing returns past about 100 ([African-language ASR benchmark](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10968); [Assamese study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.17164)).

That project is also the best case, and the hotline differs from it in costly ways. It had one speaker and one register, and its transcripts already existed, published by The Daily Sicha. The hotline draws 10–20 lectures a day from many speakers and websites and would have to pay for its labels. Correcting a machine draft costs an estimated **$72–$210 per audio hour** and transcribing from scratch $144–$420, figures built from transcriber pay rates ([Upwork](https://www.upwork.com/resources/best-transcription-jobs)) and the finding that correcting is about twice as fast as typing ([LREC 2016](https://aclanthology.org/L16-1314.pdf)). Correction stops saving time once a draft passes about 30% word error ([CMU study](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~fmetze/interACT/Publications_files/publications/asr_threshold_w4a.pdf)), a bar the free models miss on this register, so even a homegrown model would begin by buying drafts from a vendor. Computing power, by contrast, is cheap: a full experimental campaign costs roughly $10–$5,000 at current GPU rental prices ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/fine-tune-whisper-models-on-amazon-sagemaker-with-lora/); [Thunder Compute](https://www.thundercompute.com/blog/runpod-pricing-vs-thunder-compute)), and running the finished model costs $10–$60 a month ([Databricks Whisper benchmark](https://github.com/jtaylorisbell/whisper-benchmarking)). The money goes to people.

| Fine-tuning scenario | Year one, with staff time | Year one, cash only | Each later year, with staff time | Year-one payback requires an engine costing more than |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minimal: 6-hour reference set, 50 hours of corrected drafts, 6–10 developer-weeks | $30,000–$119,000 | $5,000–$15,500 | $9,000–$45,000 | $5.80–$22.90 an hour ($1.00–$3.00 counting cash only) |
| Serious: 10-hour reference set, 250 hours labeled, ~2,000 machine-labeled hours, 12–20 developer-weeks | $74,500–$281,000 | $24,000–$74,000 | $16,000–$78,000 | $14.30–$54.10 an hour ($4.60–$14.20 counting cash only) |

These are estimates assembled from the labeling, GPU and transcription rates above plus an assumed $3,000–$7,000 per developer-week. Engineering makes up half to three-quarters of the fully loaded cost, so the totals move with whoever does the work.

Set those payback prices against the cost table. At the listed (largely search-extract) prices, Yiddish Tools costs $0.05–$0.63 an hour, Gemini $0.10–$0.40 and Sofer.ai about $1.35; only YiddishLabs might cost more than $6, and its price is unknown. A homegrown model would also replace only the engine, leaving the pipeline, summaries and review page still to build. For this hotline, **the minimal fine-tune would turn a $20–$270 monthly bill into a $30,000-plus first year**. A subtler reason to wait is that fine-tuning chases verbatim accuracy, which the hotline does not need. No study has measured how much transcript error a summarizer can absorb before headings go wrong, so a model that halves word errors could leave headings no better than a rented engine already makes them.

Homegrown models also fail in ways vendors have already debugged. Whisper models retrained on short clips lose their ability to place timestamps and loop on long recordings unless trained on long-form audio ([Swiss German study](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15726v2)); retraining on one new language alone can nearly triple errors in the original one ([LoRA-Whisper](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06619v1)), a risk for lectures that mix in English; and training a model on its own transcripts produced no gain in one study and worse results in another ([arXiv 2606.07608](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.07608); [arXiv 2510.24570](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.24570)). A build takes an estimated three to six months, during which the hotline keeps paying for its current tool.

Fine-tuning becomes the right investment only under narrower conditions: the bake-off or production logs show affordable engines consistently garbling the loshon-kodesh or names of the speakers who supply most of the audio; a handful of speakers dominates the content, which brings the hotline closer to the single-speaker case that worked so well; or vendor terms rule out hotline use. Until then, two cheap steps keep the option open. Logging every staff correction and growing a glossary of sefarim, speakers and terms pays off immediately, since the glossary goes straight into the AI's instructions, and it builds training data for later. And if dependence on small vendors worries the organization, it can ask whether Yiddish Tools' $1,499 self-host license permits retraining, which would secure a strong starting model without a research project. **One question nobody has examined must be answered before any training**: whether the hotline has permission to use lectures taken from third-party websites as training data, and whether vendors allow training on the transcripts they return.

## Timestamps stay true only when the AI never sets the clock

### Software, not the AI, keeps time

The requirement that headings match the audio fails most often when an AI model is asked to write the times itself. Developers have reported Gemini timestamp problems across model generations. On an 11-minute-49-second clip, **Gemini 3 Flash's last timestamp landed near 9:14**, squeezing the timeline by about two and a half minutes, while Gemini 3.1 Pro drifted about 16 seconds by the end; Flash-Lite stayed within about a second in that test, but later posters reported drift on Flash-Lite and 3.5 Flash too ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/bug-gemini-3-flash-and-3-1-pro-progressive-timestamp-drift-in-audio-transcription/129501)). On a three-and-a-half-hour meeting, Simon Willison found that Gemini 3 Pro's "timestamps are incorrect" ([Simon Willison](https://simonw.substack.com/p/trying-out-gemini-3-pro-with-audio)), and one team's subtitles broke overnight "despite no prompt/model change" ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-api-srt-transcription-suddenly-broken-timestamps-are-wildly-inaccurate-since-yesterday-despite-no-prompt-model-change/111846)), a sign of silent server-side updates. Long files also trigger loops, with Gemini 2.5 Flash repeating a single passage dozens of times on 90-minute recordings ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/inconsistent-transcriptions-with-gemini-2-5-flash/105328)). Text-only models do no better when asked to copy times out of a long transcript: they lose exact times and skip the middle ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/masonwritescode/auto-generate-video-chapters-with-whisper-an-llm-without-hallucinated-timestamps-1oej)).

The fix, which transcription vendors use in their own products, is to let the AI choose content while software keeps time. AssemblyAI takes section times from its speech engine and never lets the language model set them ([AssemblyAI](https://www.assemblyai.com/blog/automatically-determine-video-sections-with-ai-using-python)), and Gladia checks every summary point against word timestamps and flags any it cannot match ([Gladia](https://www.gladia.io/blog/meeting-summarization-pipeline-async-stt-llm)). In the hotline's pipeline, any engine without trustworthy word times receives each lecture in pieces a minute or two long, cut at natural pauses with a few seconds of overlap, so every piece carries an exact start time and no engine can drift further than one piece; developers report the same chunking cure for Gemini's problems ([pyvideotrans](https://github.com/jianchang512/pyvideotrans/issues/624)). Each transcript line gets a number, and **the AI returns chapters as line ranges plus a short verbatim quote from each chapter's first and last lines, never as times**. Code then checks that the ranges run in order without overlapping, confirms the quotes appear where claimed, converts line numbers to times and snaps each boundary to the nearest pause. Anything that fails a check shows up as a warning in the review page instead of being silently patched.

The times themselves can come from three places, none yet measured on Yiddish, so the pilot should compare them. Yiddish Tools returns a time for every word ([Yiddish Tools](https://yiddish.tools/)). Failing that, a cheap ivrit.ai Whisper pass produces a timed transcript and the better engine's text, or the AI's quotes, is matched onto it, the method rebbe-yiddish-asr used to align farbrengen audio, anchoring 94–98% of the text ([rebbe-yiddish-asr](https://github.com/golanbenoni/rebbe-yiddish-asr)). A third route, ctc-forced-aligner, lines text up with audio in more than 1,100 languages by romanizing it, and absorbs stretches with no matching text ([ctc-forced-aligner](https://github.com/MahmoudAshraf97/ctc-forced-aligner)); the popular WhisperX aligner has a Hebrew model but none for Yiddish ([WhisperX](https://github.com/m-bain/whisperX/blob/main/whisperx/alignment.py)). Whisper-family engines loop on niggunim and silence, but the loops leave a recognizable signature, identical text in three or more consecutive segments and segments exactly one second long, that a simple filter removes ([arXiv 2603.04657](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.04657)).

### A blind staff test, not a leaderboard, picks the summarizer

A one-to-two-hour transcript fits inside current models' context windows, so the AI can read a whole lecture at once, but three short passes are easier to check than one long one. The first proposes 6–20 chapters per hour as line ranges with titles and a clip-worthiness score; the second writes each chapter's summary from that chapter's text alone; and a cheap third pass flags any summary sentence the text does not support. Splitting the work also blunts a known weakness, since errors cluster near the end of long generated summaries ([arXiv 2505.15291](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.15291)). Which model does this best for Hasidic Yiddish is unknown. The open models tested on Kashes scored 72–80 on a translation-quality scale for Yiddish into English ([MameLoshnLM/Kashes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05850)), and frontier models are likely stronger, but nobody has measured how any of them handle loshon-kodesh, Aramaic citations or garbled transcripts. A glossary of sefarim, speakers and terms in the instructions costs almost nothing and is reused on every call. English headings are the safer default, because existing evaluations measure Yiddish into English; Yiddish headings can follow once the bake-off supports them. Llama-family models are weak candidates: in a tokenizer test run for this research, Llama 3 split unpointed Yiddish into roughly three times as many pieces as OpenAI's current tokenizer ([Llama 3 tokenizer](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@lenml/tokenizer-llama3); [gpt-tokenizer](https://www.npmjs.com/package/gpt-tokenizer)), which raises cost and hints at thin Hebrew-script training data. The bake-off should run three to five models blind on identical transcripts, with two staff rating each heading correct, partly correct or wrong.

### Clips snap to pauses and leave in phone format

Cutting is the most mature step. Pauses come from Silero VAD, a free, MIT-licensed detector that processes a 30-millisecond chunk of audio in under a millisecond on one processor thread ([Silero VAD](https://github.com/snakers4/silero-vad)), cross-checked by ffmpeg's silence filter, whose defaults of −60 dB and two seconds are far too strict for speech and need tuning ([FFmpeg filters](https://github.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/blob/master/doc/filters.texi)). Each proposed boundary moves outward to the nearest pause, gains a fraction of a second of lead-in and tail with short fades, and is loudness-normalized in two passes. Phone systems commonly expect **8 kHz mono 16-bit PCM WAV**, which Sangoma requires outright with a 2 MB limit ([Sangoma](https://sangomakb.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/Fonality/pages/198803516)), while Twilio accepts MP3, WAV and μ-law among others ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/docs/voice/twiml/play)); the export should follow the hotline platform's own specification, which this research could not find, and keep a full-quality master for re-exports. Storing each clip as a start and end time, not just a file, makes any re-cut after a staff nudge instant.

The review page is a small custom web app assembled from open-source parts. The BBC's Peaks.js draws a zoomable waveform with draggable segment markers "that denote content to be clipped" ([Peaks.js](https://github.com/bbc/peaks.js)), and Hyperaudio Lite, a 10 KB MIT-licensed script, supplies a transcript in which clicking any word jumps the audio there ([Hyperaudio Lite](https://github.com/hyperaudio/hyperaudio-lite)). Keyboard shortcuts for play, set in and out, snap to pause, preview edges, merge and split keep review fast, and logging every decision yields the production accuracy figures, and future training data, at no extra cost.

## Conclusion

The question the hotline started with, which AI can understand Hasidic Yiddish, has been largely answered by a crowded and fast-moving market; the question that now decides success is how cheaply the hotline can check that AI against its own audio. That makes the reference set of 10–15 staff-outlined lectures the most valuable thing the team will build. It costs mostly staff time, outlives any vendor, and lets the hotline rerun the whole comparison in a day whenever a tool changes, which in this market happens monthly: Yiddi Type, 3Yiddish and ElevenLabs all shipped new versions in November 2026 alone ([Yiddi Type releases](https://github.com/YankyAppel/yiddi-type-releases); [3Yiddish](https://github.com/TripleU613/3Yiddish); [i-scoop](https://www.i-scoop.eu/elevenlabs-scribe-v3/)). Paired with the hotline's volume, far beyond any published plan from Sofer.ai's 90-hour Max tier ([Sofer.ai pricing](https://www.sofer.ai/#pricing)) to YiddishLabs' $149.99 top plan ([directory](https://github.com/pinta-partners/torah-yiddish-ai-directory)), the reference set turns every vendor conversation into a negotiation in which the hotline holds the scorecard.

Fine-tuning, meanwhile, is better treated as a by-product than a project. A pipeline that logs every heading staff accept or fix accumulates, at no extra cost, the kind of in-domain labeled material that made the Chabad model work, and an engine-agnostic design lets the hotline swap in a homegrown model only when those logs show it would beat the rented ones. Two unanswered legal questions, whether lectures taken from other websites may be used to train a model and whether vendors' commercial terms cover a hotline, matter more to that future than any technical choice, and both are cheap to settle now.
